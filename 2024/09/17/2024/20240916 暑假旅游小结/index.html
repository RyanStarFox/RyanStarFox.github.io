暑期旅途

2023年的暑假，自六月中旬开始，至八月中下旬的军训，我一度以为这可能是我最长的假期，也是旅游最多的假期了：我于7月与友人前去西藏，看了银河横跨星空、路过赛马比赛、赏了冰川遇了藏羚羊；于7月底去了日本，在福冈拜访了林爷爷，泡了日本温泉，喜欢上了京都这座城市，和奈良小鹿贴贴，并且在归国的飞机上发了烧；于8月重游南京，重访明孝陵，在夫子庙人挤人，又顺路去了扬州游览瘦西湖，并且在旅途中就发烧了。充实又开心，我知道，这将是我此生难忘的一个夏天。

谁知第二年——也就是今年的暑假，我因无小学期，放了足足3个月的假，从6月中到9月中，于是我竟然比去年旅游的次数还要多——这一年暑假也将是此生难忘吧？

北京

6月前去北京。上海是一座气候舒适但是没什么文化底蕴的城市，北京是一座文化底蕴深厚但是不怎么舒适的城市——感觉这是大多数北京的同学对于北京的看法。兴许是时间的缘故吧，我并未体会到北京的不舒适，仅有的几天停留中，也还没来得及吃厌北京火锅和北京烤鸭。

我是有点嫉妒北京的文化底蕴的，总觉得随便一个地方都能和历史上的某一个事件联系起来，这种与过去连接的感觉非常奇妙。虽然如果从小住在北京，或是在北京住了足够长的时间，应该会对这种感觉越来越不屑吧？

在北京拜访了几位友人，并且长谈。许久不见，总有说不完的话。与y聊天的那一夜的酒喝到近23:00，地铁停运，在这座陌生的城市里骑了一个钟头共享单车才到旅馆——清北的位置真的是偏远啊！那天晚上还路过了友校：

虽然本不想安排得非常累，但是没想到最后还是有些特种兵。最累的一天反而不是去长城的那一天——那一天早起，8点到八达岭长城的时候已经人满为患。有人抄近路乘缆车到离好汉坡只有一楼之遥的北7楼（没记错的话），我是从北一楼一路爬到了好汉坡，又一路爬到了没什么人的最最末尾北十二楼，在那里与一位大叔分别为对方合影留念，最后在北十一楼下长城。最累的是游览颐和园、圆明园和清北的那一天。颐和、圆明二园本就很大，最后也没有完全兜玩，只是确保那些必看的都去一一参观过。随后去清，不料清在自招活动之类的，未能如愿进校，前去北，leo带我兜了一圈，随后与leo前去王府井参观Apple store和它对面的Huawei store（这就是无可救药的果粉）。那一天走了3万余步，最累。

庆丰包子味道一般，但是看上去很有帝王之气。

崇明

去崇明也是临时起意，起因是身为护士的母亲难得地有了连续两天的休息，就决定前去从未去过但是不远的崇明岛。

一路开车过去的，经过上海长江大桥（可惜上海市长不是江大桥了），我妈有感而发：“这座桥值得50元路费。”

崇明两天主要就逛逛各种森林公园，吃吃便宜又美味的农家乐。农家乐的饭菜真的比商场预制菜好吃一百万倍！（是不是商场里除了烤肉和火锅都是预制菜。。。）海边有很多螃蟹。

看到东滩可以夜公园，想起来之前读到过上海最适合观星的地方是崇明，想以后带着望远镜来看星星。东滩好像是一个扬子鳄保护基地之类的，可惜没见到扬子鳄。

回家的时候意外地发现嘉定的电视塔和电线杆很日本，在车上胡乱拍了一张。

嵊泗

去嵊泗也是临时起意，原因也是身为护士的母亲难得地有了连续两天的休息。一路开车过去，经过东海大桥，我妈有感而发：“这座桥值100元路费。”

到了沈家湾码头，一路乘船去嵊泗，船蛮稳的，不晕。穿上在放奥运会。

嵊泗是一座美丽的小岛，爱上了，加入旅居城市清单了。海边真的很美，好想永远住在海边啊！

在这里租不到山地自行车，所以只能租电瓶车环岛骑行，非常舒适，只是我还是更喜欢自行车一些。

第二天早起看日出，结果没看到，有山挡着，太阳不是从海里出来的，而是从山后出来的。

海边好多小螃蟹和海蟑螂。

长沙张家界

与Leo一起去的，住在五一广场，也就是市中心，离Apple Store很近，两个无可救药的果粉在长沙Apple store体验vision pro（这已经是我第三次体验vision pro了）。

马王堆汉墓和湖南博物馆都要提前预约，我们临行前才想起，马王堆汉墓去不成了，湖南博物馆在携程上找了一个导游带我们讲解参观，导游水平很高，感觉性格很受。在湖南博物馆看到了辛追夫人，这么看来也不用去马王堆汉墓了。

在长沙呆了两天后，去了张家界。

张家界全是土菜馆，所以我也吃得很开心——美味无比！我爱土菜馆！我爱农家乐！量大管饱又美味！建议交大开满中国各地的农家乐。

张家界很奇妙，或许是阿凡达拍摄地的缘故吧，所以感觉外国人比例甚至可能超过了上海市中心，甚至是可以和交大这样的大学相当。

山真的是一重一重的。

不过我对于张家界的感知也并没有觉得景色非常美，感觉壮观归壮观，但是不过尔尔。不过值得一提的是那里的接驳车系统——原始但是有效！那里的接驳车系统如下：接驳车按照规定的路线行驶，和司机说好你要去哪里，他沿着路线放你下去之后你去找工作人员，工作人员会告诉你接下来乘坐哪一班接驳车并且告知那个司机自己的目的地。这个看上去有点原始的系统竟然出奇地高效！repect respect

我和Leo一直有一个感知：SHSer的走路速度一直比普通人快一些，尤其是对于普通人正常的速度，我们会觉得很难受，有点高不成低不就，再快一点慢一点都会舒服得多。这一点在张家界那里得到了印证：游客中心说要30分钟的路程，我们竟然只走了15分钟，对此颇为惊讶。在金鞭溪那里的时候，由于是平地，我们记了个时，发现2.58km的走路配速竟然是12'11''，还有两段7km的记录，竟然配速也是15'多，真是擅长走路的两个人。

btw，金鞭溪好多🐒。

这只猢狲看上去在深思

在机场回上海候机的时候遇到两个同去上海的人，他们像是科技控股相关公司的员工，与他们相谈甚欢，我和Leo正好专业对口，又是果粉。对方提到想投资安克，正在做调研，安克正是主流苹果配件厂商之一。

日本

重访日本，此次主要目的是带外公外婆出过玩玩——外婆还接到了太外婆的任务，买点酥心糖吃吃：太外婆记得小时候吃到的东洋糖非常甜，想吃吃看。酥心糖是没有买到，但是买了很多软糖硬糖和糕点。买的大福本还担心她咬不动，结果竟然一下子吃了8个，吃得晚饭都吃不下。

此次依次拜访了名古屋-京都-奈良-大阪。填补了一些去年的遗憾吧。

名古屋去年未去过，但感觉大名鼎鼎，去看了看名古屋城。

京都去年的遗憾很多，一一填补：在伏见稻荷大社重买了去年弄丢的狐狸玩具、进去了去年过了参拜时间而没能参观的国宝金阁寺、参观了去年根本没时间去的二条城。我发现我对京都已经开始有点熟悉了，有一夜家人都累了在酒店休息，我独自出去逛，凭我的记忆走到了去年住的酒店。在鸭川旁边坐了很久。京都也是一个以后有机会一定要来旅居的城市！加入旅居列表！

奈良重访小鹿，它们比去年凶一些，可能是秋天发情期的缘故吧？今年进了春日大社——去年在春日大社门口遇见了P同学，长谈之后竟然过了参观时间。

在大阪的第一天我又一次经过了打烊的黑门市场，但是在最后回国的那一天上午，我终于见到了开门的黑门市场！A5和牛美味。三度经过了去年吃的一家烧肉，可惜此次没机会吃，下次再去。

在日本的几天，我的外公外婆其实不是很吃得惯日本的饭菜，甚至我妈也没有很吃得惯——本以为日本与中国足够近，应该菜品也足够相近的。到头来，我的外公外婆最满意的是两顿弥生轩的日式快餐和一顿一兰拉面。

此次出行也有一些插曲，四人各犯了一次错：

我：把Apple pencil落在京都的酒店，连夜从奈良赶回京都，再回奈良几乎是乘到了JR的末班车。

母亲：约定好在A地点等我，我送疲倦的外公外婆回酒店后回到A点，竟然不见踪影，电话也打不通，此时还暴雨骤降，我只好狼狈地去罗森躲雨。后来知道她只是去逛免税店了，免税店太吵接不到电话。

外婆：上面我送外公外婆回酒店的原因不仅仅是因为他们疲倦，还因外婆的手机落在了酒店。

外公：在机场跟着我们走结果自己走散了，还好距离不远，找到了。

总之还是较为顺利，没出什么大岔子。

以及此次去日本在京都遇到了T同学，去年也在奈良遇到了P同学，真是他乡遇故知啊！

其他的一些碎碎念

其实主要想讲讲日记。

我是从2018年8月1日开始写日记的，之所以这个日期记得这么清楚，是因为这是我去新加坡马来西亚旅游出发的日子。我为什么要记日记呢？其实是害怕遗忘——我太珍视自己的记忆了——记了日记，我就可以更放心地遗忘了。（其实拍照也是一个道理啊！）

一直以来，我写日记是传统的纸笔写日记的。上了大学之后，写日记是为数不多还需要用到笔的场景，我也因此格外珍惜用纸笔写日记的机会。可是在旅途中，把日记带出去又厚重又不安全，正巧iOS17发布了手记app，我便在旅途中体验体验，在此分享一下自己的感受：

电子日记最令人顾虑的是数据，不过目前绝大多数日记平台都支持导出日记/打印日记了，这是我敢用电子日记的大前提！电子日记的最大优点是记录多媒体：手记app每次都会推荐放在日记里的图片、定位等多媒体，我很喜欢。不仅如此，还有一个记录心情功能，这个功能是我最心水的，我几乎想以后不用纸笔记日记了。

但是它也有痛点：没有iPad版，也就意味着它不能手写，我认为这就失去了日记的感觉——其实用Apple pencil写日记已经让我感到勉为其难了，但是手记只能在手机上打字，因此我放弃了这个计划。

但是我有计划将手记app和纸质日记的优点相结合：

在旅途中，我会选择手记app，不用带出日记本，安全又方便。而且旅途中会有大量照片、定位，正合适手记app。

平时，我会记录纸质日记，但我打算参考手记app的记录心情功能，给自己做一些小便签小贴纸记录每天的心情。

Summer Travels

The summer vacation of 2023 started in mid-June and lasted until the military training in mid-to-late August. I initially thought this would be my longest and most travel-intensive holiday: In July, I went to Tibet with a friend, where we saw the Milky Way stretching across the sky, attended a horse racing event, admired glaciers, and spotted Tibetan antelopes. At the end of July, I visited Japan—I met Grandpa Lin in Fukuoka, soaked in Japanese hot springs, fell in love with Kyoto, cuddled with deer in Nara, and even had a fever on the flight back to China. In August, I revisited Nanjing, explored Ming Xiaoling Mausoleum, braved the crowds at Confucius Temple, took a detour to Yangzhou to see Slender West Lake, and also developed a fever during the trip. It was a fulfilling and joyful experience; I know this will be a summer I’ll never forget.

Who would have thought that the following year—this summer—due to the absence of a short semester, I got a full three-month break, from mid-June to mid-September. As a result, I ended up traveling even more than last year! This summer will surely be unforgettable too.

Beijing

I went to Beijing in June. Shanghai is a city with a comfortable climate but little cultural heritage, while Beijing has rich cultural heritage but an uncomfortable climate—this seems to be the view of most Beijing students. Perhaps it was due to timing, but I didn’t experience Beijing’s discomfort. During my brief stay, I didn’t even get tired of eating Beijing hot pot and Peking duck.

I’m a little envious of Beijing’s cultural heritage; it feels like every place here is connected to some historical event, and that sense of connection to the past is truly magical. Of course, if one grew up in Beijing or lived there long enough, they might grow indifferent to this feeling over time.

I visited a few friends in Beijing and had long chats with them. It’s always hard to run out of things to talk about after a long separation. I drank until nearly 11 PM with Y, and when the subway stopped running, I rode a shared bike for an hour through this unfamiliar city to reach my hotel—Tsinghua and Peking University are really located in remote areas! That night, I also passed by a neighboring university:

Although I didn’t plan to over-schedule myself, I ended up having a rather packed itinerary. The most tiring day wasn’t the one we spent at the Great Wall—we left early that day, and when we arrived at Badaling Great Wall at 8 AM, it was already crowded. Some people took a shortcut and rode the cable car to North Tower 7, which is just one floor away from the "Heroes' Slope"; I climbed from North Tower 1 all the way to the Heroes' Slope, then continued to North Tower 12, where there were few people. There, I took a photo with an uncle as a souvenir, and finally descended from North Tower 11. The most exhausting day was when we visited the Summer Palace, Old Summer Palace, and Tsinghua/Peking University. The Summer Palace and Old Summer Palace are both vast, and we didn’t explore them fully, but we made sure to visit all the must-see spots. Later, we tried to visit Tsinghua, but it was hosting an admissions event, so we couldn’t enter. We then went to Peking University, where Leo showed me around. After that, Leo and I visited the Apple Store and the Huawei Store across the street (typical die-hard Apple fans!). We walked over 30,000 steps that day—truly exhausting.

Qingfeng Baozi tasted average, but it looked quite regal.

Chongming

My trip to Chongming was spontaneous. My mother, who is a nurse, had two consecutive days off, so we decided to visit Chongming Island, a place we had never been to but was nearby.

We drove there via the Shanghai Yangtze River Bridge (too bad the mayor of Shanghai isn’t Jiang Daqiao!). My mom remarked, “This bridge is worth the 50 yuan toll.”

We spent two days in Chongming exploring various forest parks and enjoying cheap, delicious farmhouse meals. Farmhouse food is a million times better than pre-made dishes from supermarkets! (Are all pre-made dishes in supermarkets just frozen food...?) There were lots of crabs by the sea.

I heard that Dongtan has a night park, and since I read that Chongming is the best place for stargazing in Shanghai, I plan to bring a telescope there someday. Dongtan seems to be a conservation area for Chinese alligators, but we didn’t see any.

On the way home, I was surprised to find that the TV tower and utility poles in Jiading looked very Japanese; I snapped a quick photo from the car.

Shengsi

Our trip to Shengsi was also spontaneous, again because my mother had two consecutive days off as a nurse. We drove there via the Donghai Bridge, and my mom commented, “This bridge is worth 100 yuan in tolls!”

We arrived at Shenjiawan Wharf and took a ferry to Shengsi. The ride was smooth, and we didn’t get seasick. The Olympics were being broadcast on board.

Shengsi is a beautiful island; I fell in love with it and added it to my list of places to live permanently. The seaside is truly stunning—I wish I could live there forever!

We couldn’t rent mountain bikes there, so we opted for electric scooters to circle the island—it was very comfortable, though I still prefer regular bicycles.

We woke up early the next day to watch the sunrise, but unfortunately, we didn’t see it because a mountain blocked the view; the sun rose behind the mountain instead of over the sea.

There were lots of small crabs and sea cockroaches by the beach.

Changsha & Zhangjiajie

I went to Changsha and Zhangjiajie with Leo. We stayed in Wuyi Square, the city center, which is close to the Apple Store. Two die-hard Apple fans, we tried out the Vision Pro at the Changsha Apple Store (this was my third time trying it).

Visits to Mawangdui Han Tomb and Hunan Museum require advance booking. We realized this only after arriving; we couldn’t visit Mawangdui Han Tomb, so we hired a tour guide via Ctrip to show us around Hunan Museum. The guide was excellent and very knowledgeable. We saw Lady Xinzhui at Hunan Museum, so visiting Mawangdui Han Tomb became unnecessary.

After spending two days in Changsha, we headed to Zhangjiajie.

Zhangjiajie is full of local restaurants, so I enjoyed the food—delicious! I love local restaurants and farmhouse meals! They’re generous, filling, and tasty! I recommend adding farmhouse meals from all over China to my list of favorites.

Zhangjiajie is quite unique; perhaps because it was the filming location for Avatar, the proportion of foreigners there might even exceed that of downtown Shanghai, comparable to a university like Jiaotong University.

The mountains here are layered upon each other.

However, I didn’t find the scenery in Zhangjiajie particularly breathtaking; it was impressive, but nothing extraordinary. One thing worth mentioning, though, is their shuttle bus system—simple yet effective! The system works like this: shuttles follow designated routes; you tell the driver where you want to go, and once he drops you off along the route, you ask the staff for the next shuttle and inform the driver your destination. This seemingly primitive system is surprisingly efficient! Respect, respect.

Leo and I have always noticed that SJTU students walk faster than average people. For us, normal walking speed feels uncomfortable; we prefer to walk either faster or slower. This was evident in Zhangjiajie: the visitor center said a certain route would take 30 minutes, but we covered it in just 15 minutes, which surprised us. While walking along Jinbianxi, since it’s flat, we timed ourselves and found that we covered 2.58 km in 12 minutes and 11 seconds. We also had two segments of 7 km, which took around 15 minutes each—what a pair of fast walkers!

By the way, there were lots of monkeys at Jinbianxi.

This monkey looked deep in thought.

While waiting for our flight back to Shanghai at the airport, we met two people heading to Shanghai as well. They worked for a tech-related company, and we had a great chat. Leo and I matched their major interests and were both Apple fans. They mentioned wanting to invest in Anker, which is a leading Apple accessory manufacturer, and were currently doing market research.

Japan

I revisited Japan, mainly to take my grandparents on a trip—my grandmother even had a mission from her own mother to buy some sugar candies to taste: her mother remembered how sweet the candies she ate as a child were and wanted to try them again. We didn’t buy the sugar candies, but we bought lots of soft candies, hard candies, and pastries. We worried my grandmother wouldn’t be able to chew the mochi we bought, but she ate 8 pieces in one go and couldn’t finish dinner afterward.

We visited Nagoya, Kyoto, Nara, and Osaka in sequence, making up for some of last year’s missed opportunities.

We hadn’t been to Nagoya last year, but it’s quite famous, so we went to see Nagoya Castle.

We had many regrets in Kyoto last year, which we made up for this time: we repurchased the fox toy we lost last year at Fushimi Inari Shrine, visited Kinkaku-ji Temple, a national treasure we couldn’t enter due to closing time last year, and explored Nijo Castle, which we didn’t have time for last year. I feel like I’m getting familiar with Kyoto. One night, when everyone else was tired and resting at the hotel, I went out alone based on my memory and found my way back to the hotel where we stayed last year. I sat by the Kamo River for a long time. Kyoto is another city I’d love to visit and live in someday! Added to my list of places to live!

We revisited the deer in Nara; they seemed more aggressive this year, probably due to the mating season in autumn. This year, we entered Kasuga Taisha Shrine—last year, we met classmate P at the entrance to Kasuga Taisha and talked for so long that we missed the visiting hours.

On our first day in Osaka, I passed by the closed Kuromon Market again, but on the morning of our departure, I finally saw it open! A5 Wagyu beef was delicious. We passed by a yakiniku restaurant we ate at last year, but unfortunately, we didn’t have a chance to dine there this time—maybe next time.

During our stay in Japan, my grandparents weren’t used to Japanese food, and my mom wasn’t either—I thought Japanese and Chinese cuisine would be similar enough. In the end, my grandparents preferred two meals at Yayoi-ken, a Japanese fast-food chain, and one bowl of Ramen Ichiran.

There were also a few mishaps during this trip, with each family member making a mistake once:

Me: I left my Apple Pencil at the Kyoto hotel. I rushed back to Kyoto from Nara overnight and took almost the last JR train back to Nara.

Mother: She was supposed to meet me at point A after I took my tired grandparents back to the hotel. When I arrived at point A, she was nowhere to be found, and her phone was unreachable. It was pouring rain, so I had to take shelter at a Lawson store. Later, we found out she had gone shopping at a duty-free store, where it was too noisy to hear her phone.

Grandmother: The reason I took my grandparents back to the hotel wasn’t just because they were tired; her phone was also left at the hotel.

Grandfather: He followed us at the airport but got separated; fortunately, he wasn’t far away, and we found him quickly.

Overall, everything went smoothly without any major issues.

Oh, and during this trip to Japan, I met classmate T in Kyoto, and last year I met P in Nara—what a coincidence to meet old friends abroad!

Miscellaneous Thoughts

I mainly want to talk about diaries.

I started writing a diary on August 1, 2018, a date I remember so clearly because it was the day I departed for a trip to Singapore and Malaysia. Why do I keep a diary? It’s to avoid forgetting—I cherish my memories too much. By keeping a diary, I can forget with peace of mind. (Photography serves the same purpose!)

Until now, I’ve written diaries the traditional way, with pen and paper. Since starting university, writing a diary is one of the few occasions when I still use a pen, so I treasure those moments even more. However, carrying a diary while traveling is bulky and unsafe. Coincidentally, iOS 17 launched the Notes app, so I tried it during my trip and would like to share my thoughts:

The biggest concern about digital diaries is data safety, but most diary platforms today support exporting or printing entries, which is why I feel comfortable using a digital diary! The greatest advantage of digital diaries is the ability to record multimedia: the Notes app often suggests images, locations, and other multimedia to include in the diary, which I really like. There’s also a mood-tracking feature, which I love the most; I almost want to stop using paper diaries altogether.

But it has its drawbacks: there’s no iPad version, meaning you can’t write by hand, which takes away the essence of diary writing. Writing with an Apple Pencil already felt forced for me, but since the Notes app only allows typing on an iPhone, I gave up on this idea.

However, I plan to combine the advantages of the Notes app and paper diaries:

During travels, I’ll use the Notes app—no need to carry a diary, making it safe and convenient. Plus, there are plenty of photos and locations to record, which fits the Notes app perfectly.

At home, I’ll continue writing paper diaries, but I plan to adopt the mood-tracking feature from the Notes app by using small sticky notes to record my daily mood.
