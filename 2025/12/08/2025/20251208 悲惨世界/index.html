从初中历史课第一次听到10周年版的《悲惨世界》序曲开始，就一直喜欢这部音乐剧作品。高中毕业后，有幸曾在23年上海文化广场看过法语版《悲惨世界》，后来又在24年上影节的院线看了电影版，25年的上影节则在院线看了10周年版的录像，今天终于有幸在上海大剧院观看40周年版的《悲惨世界》音乐会。

我自己的喜好，对于视频部分，必然是电影版最佳——毕竟有很多大制作特效，但是电影版的唱功肉耳可听不如音乐会版，真的很希望未来某一天AI能在把10周年的音频无违和地嫁接到电影版上。对于声音，我还是最喜欢10周年版本的——25周年整体的速度过快，少了庄重；法语版少了几首歌，剧情显得有点不那么连贯了。因此我最熟悉的也是10周年版的，下面的对比绝大多数也都是和10周年版本对比。

和之前一样，我也希望在观看演出前，先重听一遍10周年的专辑——但是很不巧，我这周只带了AirPods Pro回家，没法使用大馒头4听——本来多层次的音乐都被压扁了，低音的信息也丧失了，非常难受。24年上影节电影节版之前我重看了一遍原著，但是这次来不及重看一遍原著了——看了这么多遍，我想我应该也还记得大多数细节。

我这次斥巨资购置了座位非常好的票，坐在5V排正中。实际上第1，2排正中没有排座位，那里有一个延伸至台下的小T台，所以观看的时候可以离演员非常近，甚至有时恍惚间可以同时听到演员自己发出和经过麦克风收声发出的声音。序幕中冉阿让撕碎黄身份证的时候，站在T台往前扔，甚至前面两排的观众还可以捡到。有很多情绪化的表演，包括冉阿让重生、冉阿让归罪、沙威自杀、德纳第夫妇亮相，演员都会走到T台处与前排观众有眼神互动，真的能让人更加沉浸于音乐剧本身，更共情于各个角色。

我本来一直担心40周年版的演出会继承25周年版的速度过快的问题，但最终的节奏蛮符合我自己的胃口的。印象里23年的法大悲是提前录制的伴奏，这次则是现场演出，音质油好了不少。40周年的演出增添了不少的桥段（相对于10周年）。感觉开头冉阿让独白的编曲改了，变得更有层次，也与冉阿让的独白更加对应了，当然也可能是现场版音质更好。在伽弗洛什出场的时候，还增加了伽弗洛什对爱潘妮的介绍，随后沙威也重新登场介绍自己在巴黎的角色。之后还增加了德纳第夫妇在路上认出冉阿让，引来沙威，沙威驱散众人。最后婚礼前，增加了冉阿让向马吕斯介绍自己背景、德纳第夫妇阴差阳错告诉马吕斯自己是被冉阿让所救的桥段。整体上还感觉德纳第夫妇的片段变长、变多了，时不时跳出来逗逗观众，贱贱地活跃活跃气氛。演出中一些性相关的桥段的呈现比之前看的更加露骨，真的很让人担心会被删减掉，好在没有。

我对于演员和演员的唱功实际上没那么敏感，我听上去总的来说还是非常不错的，最重要的冉阿让和沙威都没有让我失望。有点可惜沙威的长发并非像10周年一样是在台上自己散开来的，而是在放冉阿让走之前台下就散开来了。我还是很想看沙威“香消玉损”自己散开长发的场面的。几位热血青年在第一幕并没有穿上他们最标志性的服装，我一开始还以为这次的服装换掉了——尤其是安灼拉标志性的红金服装，但是在第二幕就换上最熟悉的那几身衣服。这次的芳汀是一位黑人演员——其实一开始有点出戏，因为原著中的芳汀是金发美女。我觉得芳汀比较慢热，没有马上进入状态，前几句的换气非常明显，有点影响体验，但是还好最终调整过来了。芳汀是黑人，那珂赛特的演员岂不是也得是黑人——还真是。比较脸盲，开始还以为是一人分饰两角，直至最后一幕两人同时上场才意识到是两个人。这次的爱潘妮和小柯赛特竟然是华人？小柯赛特超级可爱！这一版的德纳第夫妇比较出彩，更“油”、更要面子爱显摆、更“贱”：到T台问观众讨掌声、让沙威在捉到冉阿让的时候别忘了是自己举报的、最后婚礼中用中文对观众说的“你好”、“谢谢”，忍俊不禁。

大悲的舞美也真的豪华——提几个印象比较深的点：舞台上面真的吊了一个“街垒”，在第一幕中街垒是升上去的，下面是灯，第二幕中降下来了。使用闪烁的白色灯光代表子弹，也用这种形式细细演绎了ABC之友社之死。在爱潘妮送信和沙威自杀时降下来的幽幽的路灯——真的让人感到是在阴暗的巴黎街头。以及最后的那两排蓝白红的条状灯——真的让人激情澎湃。还有最后Finale中，能明显感觉到死去的人的声音有一个“更远”的幽灵音效。但我觉得也有两点不如法大悲：法大悲在卞福汝主角出场时，用白光在舞台上方投影出了一个巨大的白色十字架，强调了他真的就是一个善良的“上帝”的化身。法大悲中多次拿出来法国国旗，尤其是在Finale中ABC之友挥舞几面法国国旗，真的让人激情澎湃——不过让一群英国人在中国挥舞法国国旗可能也有点奇怪吧。

其实本来很期待有一个大Encore的——10周年的多国冉阿让、多语言《Do you hear the people sing》和25周年的历代演员都非常不错，但想必也是首演场的专属。谁想到这一次竟然一个Encore都没有，23年的法大悲都有encore，虽然不是《Do you hear the people sing》。略失望，但无妨。最好的大悲永远是现场的大悲——期待50周年巡演，我想我应当是还会再买票的。

I first fell in love with Les Misérables during middle school when I heard the overture from the 10th-anniversary edition in history class. After graduating high school, I was fortunate enough to watch the French-language version at Shanghai Culture Square in 2023, then saw the film adaptation in theaters during the Shanghai International Film Festival in 2024. In 2025, I watched a recording of the 10th-anniversary edition in theaters during the festival, and today I finally got to see the 40th-anniversary concert version of Les Misérables at Shanghai Grand Theatre.

In terms of video adaptations, the film version is undoubtedly the best for me—it features many elaborate special effects. However, the vocal performances in the film are inferior to those in the concert version. I truly hope that one day AI can seamlessly integrate the audio from the 10th-anniversary edition into the film version. When it comes to sound quality, my favorite remains the 10th-anniversary version: the 25th-anniversary edition is played too quickly, lacking the solemnity of the original; the French version omits several songs, making the plot feel less coherent. Since I am most familiar with the 10th-anniversary version, most of my comparisons below will be against it.

As usual, I wanted to re-listen to the 10th-anniversary album before the show. Unfortunately, I only brought my AirPods Pro home this week and could not use my Big Muff 4 to play the music—the multi-layered melodies were flattened, and the bass details were lost, which was very disappointing. I reread the original novel before watching the 2024 film festival version, but I did not have time to do so again this time. Having read it so many times, I believe I still remember most of the details.

This time, I splurged on a ticket with an excellent seat, right in the middle of row 5V. In fact, there are no seats in the middle of rows 1 and 2; instead, there is a small T-stage extending towards the audience. This allows viewers to be extremely close to the actors, and at times, you can even hear both the actors’ live voices and the amplified microphone sounds simultaneously. During the opening scene when Jean Valjean tears up his yellow passport, he throws it from the T-stage, and audience members in the first two rows can even pick it up. Many emotionally charged performances, including Jean Valjean’s rebirth, his confession of guilt, Javert’s suicide, and the Thénardiers’ entrance, feature the actors stepping onto the T-stage to make eye contact with the front-row audience. This truly immerses viewers in the musical and helps them empathize with the characters.

I had been worried that the 40th-anniversary performance would inherit the overly fast tempo of the 25th-anniversary version, but the pacing ended up suiting my preferences perfectly. I recall that the French version in 2023 used pre-recorded accompaniment, while this time it was a live performance, resulting in much better sound quality. The 40th-anniversary show added several new segments compared to the 10th-anniversary version. It seems the arrangement of Jean Valjean’s monologue at the beginning was changed to be more layered and better match his words; of course, it could also be due to the superior sound quality of the live version. When Gavroche appears, a new segment was added where he introduces Éponine, followed by Javert’s reappearance to introduce his role in Paris. Later, the Thénardiers recognize Jean Valjean on the road, prompting Javert to intervene and disperse the crowd. Before the wedding, Jean Valjean introduces his background to Marius, and the Thénardiers accidentally tell Marius that they were the ones who saved him. Overall, the Thénardier scenes were longer and more frequent, with the couple frequently popping up to tease the audience and liven up the atmosphere. Some sexually suggestive scenes in this version were more explicit than what I had seen before, which made me worry they might be cut, but fortunately they remained intact.

I am not particularly sensitive to the actors’ singing abilities, and overall, the performances were very good. Both Jean Valjean and Javert did not disappoint me. It’s a shame that Javert’s long hair did not spread out on stage as it did in the 10th-anniversary version; instead, it was already loose before Jean Valjean left. I really wanted to see Javert’s hair spread out dramatically as he met his demise. The group of revolutionaries did not wear their signature costumes in Act 1; I initially thought the costumes had been changed—especially Enjolras’ iconic red and gold outfit—but they switched to their familiar attire in Act 2. Fantine was played by a Black actress; at first, I found it a bit jarring, as the original novel describes Fantine as a blonde beauty. Fantine seemed to take a while to warm up, with noticeable breathing issues in her first few lines, which affected the experience slightly, but she eventually adjusted. Since Fantine was Black, Cosette’s actress was also Black—indeed. I have a poor memory for faces and initially thought they were the same person playing two roles; I realized they were different only when both appeared together in the final act. Éponine and little Cosette were actually of Chinese descent! Little Cosette was incredibly adorable! The Thénardiers in this version were particularly standout: more sly, more obsessed with showing off, and more cheeky. They asked the audience for applause from the T-stage, reminded Javert to acknowledge that they were the ones who tipped him off about Jean Valjean, and even said “hello” and “thank you” in Chinese to the audience during the wedding scene—it was hilarious.

The stage design of Les Misérables is truly luxurious—here are a few highlights: a real “barricade” was suspended above the stage; in Act 1, it was raised with lights underneath, and in Act 2, it was lowered. Flashing white lights represented bullets, and this effect was used to vividly depict the demise of the Friends of ABC. The dim streetlights that descended during Éponine’s letter delivery and Javert’s suicide scene truly made the audience feel like they were in the dark streets of Paris. And those final rows of blue-white-red striped lights were incredibly inspiring. During the finale, it was clear that the voices of the deceased had a “distant” ghostly effect. However, I found two aspects inferior to the French version: in the French version, when Bishop Myriel appeared, a giant white cross was projected above the stage using white light, emphasizing that he was truly a kind-hearted incarnation of “God.” The French version also featured the French flag multiple times, especially in the finale when the Friends of ABC waved several French flags, which was very moving—though it might seem a bit strange to see a group of Brits waving the French flag in China.

I had originally hoped for a grand encore—the multi-language Jean Valjean performances, the multilingual “Do You Hear the People Sing?”, and the lineup of actors from previous editions in the 10th-anniversary and 25th-anniversary versions were all fantastic, but these were likely exclusive to the premiere. To my surprise, there was no encore at all this time; even the French version in 2023 had an encore, though it was not “Do You Hear the People Sing?” I was a little disappointed, but it doesn’t matter. The best version of Les Misérables is always the live performance—I look forward to the 50th-anniversary tour and will definitely buy a ticket again.
