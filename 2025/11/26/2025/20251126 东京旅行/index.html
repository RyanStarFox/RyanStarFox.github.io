印象

暑假快结束的时候，我意识到我整个8月都没有出去，心里闷得慌，从那时，我就开始谋划这次在学期中的旅行。对我来说，旅行的一大意义在于“陌生感”。我出生在上海，因此很难在城市中找到陌生感——很不巧，自然景观往往交通不便，对于一个学生的中短期旅途非常麻烦。因此我将目光投到国外，希望从异域中找到陌生感——最终我选择了东京作为旅行的目的地：尽管我曾两度前往日本关西地区旅游，但却从未去过日本首都、亚洲第一城市东京，此番正好弥补如此憾事。

印象中，东京是一座让人迷乱的城市——夜色中的霓虹灯、匆忙汹涌的人潮、闪烁如星光LED灯带。或许有点类似上海的疏远，但是却又有种类似于香港的江湖气。怀揣着对异域的印象与期待，我于21日上午9时，踏入了飞往东京的飞机。

旅程流水账

中午降落在成田机场，与朋友在机场的吉野家吃了在日本的第一顿正餐，随后便赶往酒店。一切收拾好之后，竟然4点多已近日落——成日在室内学习工作，竟然已经忘了冬天的黄昏有多早。我们爬上酒店的楼顶，在那里可以看到黄昏中的东京塔。东京塔仍未亮灯，旁边高楼镜子一般的外立面将黄昏的阳光反射过来，也不刺眼。东京塔微微亮灯之后，我们下楼前往东京塔下兜了一圈——我们没有上东京塔，一方面觉得东京塔本质上和东方明珠没什么区别，另一方面上了塔就看不到塔了——或许有点本末倒置。

晚间我们前往上野公园和阿美横丁。朋友没吃过鸟贵族，就去吃了一顿鸟贵族。本来是想去上野公园看红叶和黄叶的，但是晚间所有的叶子都被灯光染成温暖的黄色了。圣诞活动已经开始了，有些树上的LED灯带已经全部点亮了，仿佛奏响了80年代的迪斯科一样。

第二日前往浅草与晴空塔。晴空塔和东京塔本质上也差不多，所以我们也没上去。倒是在浅草寺和旁边的商店街逛了很久，给国内的友人带了一些御守作为伴手礼。去浅草寺求了签。朋友抽到了凶签，系在了寺里。我有些忐忑，但最终抽出来是吉签，将签纸带回了家。在浅草商店街吃了日语老师推荐的*烧肉王（焼肉きんぐ）*非常美味的烤肉自助，虽然小贵，但是吃了很多，当成两顿饭吃了。晚上去皇居外苑兜了一圈——依旧是被灯光染黄的黄叶，所幸是银杏叶，被染黄也不显违和。皇居外围有一些公园是可以进去的，但是里面是不能进的。其实给我的感受只是一个更大、护城河还没干、没有天守阁、仍在使用的日本传统城池。

第三日，就是最让人期待的富士山了。之前为了图快，我们购置了从新宿到河口湖的特急巴士票。但是措手不及的堵车，让我们花了两个半小时才到达河口湖。风景确实秀美，东京郊区的山都是午夜六色的，多い素晴らしい紅葉ですね！我们运气很好——早上起来的时候是阴天，不过在开过来的半路，太阳露出了一个角，到了傍晚终于完全放晴，像个贪睡的大学生一样。中午看着被云层笼罩的富士山，以为山上没有雪，但是放晴之后看清楚了，原来富士山上已经有一些雪了。我带了一个胶片相机，36张照片，几乎全是在富士山这里拍掉的。晚上回东京市里又是三个半小时，本想去日语老师推荐的另一家餐馆登亭去吃鳗鱼饭，但是因为堵车，错过了登亭的营业时间。本想转战24h的一兰拉面，但是发现一兰拉面在深夜的11点仍然是一座难求，只好作罢，最后在买了个鲷鱼烧，又去全家买了点热的，凑合一顿——没想到全家的鸡排与肉包味道竟然比国内的好吃这么多，吃得反倒远超预期，相当满足。

最后一日本想去吉卜力博物馆的，但在前一夜得知那里需要至少提前4天预约，只好作罢。我和朋友由于飞机班次不同，也想去不同的地方的缘故，打算分开游玩。我去了离酒店很近的两个日式庭院——旧滨离宫庭院和旧芝离宫恩赐庭园。又去了趟芝公园看了看白天的东京塔。两个日式庭院很安静，游人不多，看着远处的高楼大厦，耳边竟然没有喧嚣，只有清寥的鸟鸣，心也跟着安静下来。第一反应是，为什么国内的公园打理得不如这些庭院，后来想起来实际上家旁边的秋霞圃、汇龙潭其实也是类似的城市幽处——我应该也多去去这些地方。

本想最后一顿中饭去吃前一天没来得及吃的登亭或者一兰拉面，但一看时间，只好草草赶到成田机场，在成田机场吃了中饭。走上飞机，回望这个小假期，又坚定地走回了平常的生活。成田机场给航空公司准备的飞机餐非常好吃，也是最后的小惊喜了。生活照旧，一切如故。

胶囊酒店

本来打算一个人去旅游的，订完机酒之后，朋友才打算和我一起去，因此我们虽然是两个人一起去，但是住的还是胶囊酒店——这对solo trip是最划算的。第一次了解胶囊酒店，是在up主庄子的视频里。胶囊酒店是日本的特色的住宿模式，虽然本质上就是宿舍间，但通过一些视频了解到胶囊酒店还是打理得不错。此外，会有很多solo trip的外国人住在胶囊酒店，可以和来自世界各地的旅客交流，像是世界青旅一样。因此想试一试。

我选择的胶囊酒店是一家连锁店9h nine hours。我选择了港区滨松店，因为那家店的楼顶可以看到东京塔。Check in的时候，店员给了毛巾拖鞋，耳塞可以自取。得知我们是第一次来住宿之后，店员给了我们中文版的须知，之后我们就上楼了。

酒店一共有10楼，男女从电梯开始，就是分开的。男生可去的楼层是2，4，5，7，8，10。

  • 2楼是行李室和公共浴室、水槽。还有4个卫生间。公共浴室和水槽非常充足，我住了几天从来没有碰到过排队的情况。浴室也很大，干湿分离，有充足的空间放置衣物。行李室中，每个人有一个小格子。可以把自己的箱子放在小格子下面的空位里，放不下的话可以寄存前台。格子有点小，放自己的包和衣物有点不方便。
  • 4，5，7，8层是卧室，我住在4楼的一个上铺406。铺位的小格子其实挺大的，人可以坐直，灯光可调亮暗，也可以拉上床帘，美中不足的是没什么可以放置随身物件的地方，插座也只有一个，最好需要自备拖线板。铺位的隔音其实不是很好，能听到隔壁床的鼾声——这就有免费提供的耳塞的用武之地了。铺位的东西每天早上是要清空的，清洁工会来打扫，第二天晚上可以在床上找到新的睡衣和毛巾。睡衣对我来说有点小，我从来没有穿过，最后全部还给了前台。卧室楼层有一个小阳台。卫生间的隔间外还有一个独立的门，那扇门的开关不会发出任何声音，非常安静。住客基本上也都非常文明，整个卧室楼层是非常安静的。唯一我感到略微不文明的是，几乎每天早上都会被一个闹钟闹醒——有且仅有一个，而且还几次那个闹钟响了好久，估计是没有把主任闹醒。大多数人都是开震动闹钟的。
  • 10层是lounge，有插座、桌椅。这里一直有很多人，但是位置是充足的，总能找到空位。这里大家也都挺安静的，像是在咖啡店里一样，或许是不太熟的缘故吧。10楼有一个楼梯可以通往天台，远眺东京塔——但是视角没有预想的好。天台的座位是金属的，有点冷，人不多，因此在天台上反而更容易开启一段对话。

checkout的时候，直接把房卡放在前台就行。但这个酒店在退房后不能寄存行李——虽然实际上也没有人管，但是作为一个高素质的中国人，我还是把行李寄到了车站——几乎每个车站都能寄行李。

遇到的人

日本是个国际化的城市，我住的酒店又是类似青旅的胶囊酒店，所以我确实非常期待与来自世界各地的人交流。得益于我朋友的外向，确实也和很多人聊上了。

第一位聊上是第一晚回酒店的地铁上。我朋友在做冥想练习，不经意间与对面的女士对上了眼，那位日本女士又看到免税袋里的chikawa，我们便聊了起来。这一段聊天，我们没有聊什么深刻的内容，我们尝试用自己蹩脚的日语交流，磕磕绊绊，但也能互相听得懂。那位女士祝我们旅途顺利，但事实上也因为我们的这段聊天，让我们不小心坐过了站。

这一晚我和朋友还上了天台看东京塔，在那里和一个沙特阿拉伯年轻人攀谈了起来。那位年轻人本科毕业就工作了，现在他在放他的年假。他的年假有20天，他全部拿来到日本旅游了。我们很惊讶他有20天的年假，他主张中国应该也有。但事实上，最后查下来，中国人工作2-10年，10-20年，20年以上分别只有5，10，15天的法定年假。我们聊到了学业，我说我可能还要去博士。他说他认为工作的经验比在学校里读书重要，因此他早早地出来工作了。其实对这个观点我比较认同，因为我也感受到在学校里学习的效率其实是非常低的，但是我向他解释，在中国如果没有硕士或者博士的学历，是很难找到一份好工作的，因此即使我认同他的观点，我也不得不读完博士再去工作。

第一天晚上，我朋友发现自己手机的sim卡出了些问题，需要重复取出再装入sim卡。可惜很不巧，他的取卡针弄丢了。我建议他去楼下的docomo问一问。第二天一早我们就去docomo了。说来好笑，docomo的营业厅里写着巨大的“不出售游客版sim卡”。本来想排队，但是又来了一位店员。我们问他能不能送或者买一个取卡针，他一开始拒绝了。但是当我们告诉他我朋友的手机有点问题，需要时不时拿出取卡针的时候，他犹豫了一下，从那一串取卡针中拿了一个取卡针送给了我们。“Give you.”他为我们破了例。

第二天晚上在天台上又碰到了沙特小哥，但是没多聊。和一个英国人聊了很多。这位英国人看上去三十来岁，是一个汽修工。他说他来东京，飞了14个小时。老牌发达国家还是富有啊，连汽修工都可以在距自己国家14小时飞机的地方旅游2个礼拜。这不是他第一次来东京，他很喜欢东京，甚至有定居的想法，可惜语言不是他的强项，学不会日语。和他聊了很多。他认为不论政府如何，百姓总归都是好的——包括英国百姓、日本百姓、中国百姓。他觉得日本发展的很快，他去年来住的也是这个酒店，那是旁边的大楼还没开始造，现在已经快造好了——我们笑笑，说其实中国基建的速度更夸张，只不过中国建筑的设计确实也不如日本。他是汽修工，于是我们也和他聊了很多汽车相关的。他提到了奇瑞想要进军英国，虽然在中国人心目中，奇瑞是低端车，但是他说奇瑞在英国想冲击高端。他为沃尔沃工作（现在沃尔沃被奇瑞收购了），他说同样的配置，奇瑞比沃尔沃便宜很多，他对此有点感兴趣。我们也聊到了电动车到底环不环保——有种说法，西方不发展电车是因为电本身的生产也不环保。这位英国人认为电车是环保的——至少不排尾气。

第三天我们去了富士山。在富士山拍照的时候，我们遇到了另一波中国游客。我们互相为对方拍拍立得。我和朋友还被错认称同性情侣。在一个红叶很漂亮的机位，基本日本老奶奶和日本阿姨经过，主动提出可以给我和朋友拍合照。她们的英语不太流利，我们的日语也非常蹩脚，就只能打着手势又夹着日语和英语交流。但看得出她们非常和善友好，也非常欢迎我们来旅游——即使中日政府之间交恶，老百姓之间还是友好亲善的。临走时，朋友在沙滩边和一个法国人聊上了天。这是这位法国人的毕业旅行，他独自在日本待了近一个月。他周一离开，和我们一样，要先飞到上海，再转机回国。那天晚上我和朋友回去，路上堵车，最终流落到新桥找晚饭吃。看到新桥的一兰拉面也在半夜大排长龙，我和朋友愣住了。这时一位中国女性听到我们用中文交流，在和我们搭讪，说一兰拉面现在反而是饭店。于是我们开始在大众点评上搜索别的饭店。这时候她问我们喝酒嘛？我说不不不，只是来找晚饭吃。我这才突然意识到，原来她和她旁边的朋友，实际上是站街女。估计她也是有些尴尬，告诉我们“那里吃的多”，于是我们就分别了。她认识挺好的，只不过以这种方式认识实在有些尴尬。

最后一天，倒是没在日本遇见什么印象深刻的人，只记得在登机口的日本官员会用中文说：“谢谢。”下了飞机之后，在浦东机场照机连线的入口——指引真的是做得非常差。一位上海老太太也在找，于是我们搭话起来，后面也一起坐机连线聊天。她看上去就是那种老知识分子，举止穿着非常优雅。她的儿子移民德国，这次是在浦东机场送孙子去德国。和她聊起中日，她主张：“出过国，见过外面的人，都不会觉得这是什么事儿。”我深以为然。忘记和她聊了什么了，但印象里她的举止、她的观点，都让我感到我也想成为这样的人。

感受

去东京前，我一直对于东京是“亚洲第一大城市”感到不服气。总觉得上海、香港、新加坡都能扳扳手腕，但是去了之后才真的服气了。东京的楼宇不高，但是市区的面积非常广。东京没有淡季，即使我在11月去，新宿街头依旧游人如织。东京的国际化也是上海香港甚至是新加坡不能及的——真的能看到全家的店员有黑人有白人。上海与东京还是有很大差距的。道阻且长！

Impressions

As the summer vacation was coming to an end, I realized I hadn’t gone anywhere throughout August, which left me feeling restless. That’s when I started planning this mid-semester trip. For me, a major appeal of traveling is the sense of unfamiliarity it brings. Having been born in Shanghai, it’s hard to find that sense of unfamiliarity in cities. Unfortunately, natural landscapes often require inconvenient transportation, making them impractical for short-term trips by a student like me. So I turned my attention overseas, hoping to find that sense of unfamiliarity in a foreign land. Eventually, I chose Tokyo as my destination: although I had visited the Kansai region of Japan twice before, I had never been to Tokyo, the capital of Japan and the largest city in Asia. This trip was the perfect opportunity to make up for that.

In my mind, Tokyo is a bewildering city—neon lights at night, bustling crowds, and LED light strips twinkling like stars. It’s somewhat similar to Shanghai in its impersonal vibe, but also has a certain “gangster-like” atmosphere reminiscent of Hong Kong. With these impressions and expectations of a foreign land in mind, I boarded a plane to Tokyo at 9 a.m. on the 21st.

Trip Diary

I landed at Narita Airport at noon, had my first meal in Japan at Yoshinoya in the airport with a friend, and then headed to the hotel. After settling in, it was already past 4 p.m. and sunset was approaching—having spent all day studying and working indoors, I had forgotten how early winter evenings come. We climbed to the rooftop of the hotel, where we could see Tokyo Tower in the twilight. Tokyo Tower wasn’t lit up yet; the mirror-like facades of the surrounding skyscrapers reflected the evening sunlight without being glaring. After Tokyo Tower lit up slightly, we went downstairs and took a stroll around it. We didn’t go up Tokyo Tower—partly because we felt it was essentially no different from the Oriental Pearl Tower, and partly because if we went up, we wouldn’t be able to see the tower itself, which seemed a bit counterproductive.

In the evening, we went to Ueno Park and Ameyoko Market. My friend, who had never tried Torikun, had a meal there. We originally wanted to see the red and yellow leaves in Ueno Park, but at night, all the leaves were dyed a warm yellow by the lights. Christmas activities had already started, and some trees had their LED light strips fully lit, creating an atmosphere reminiscent of 1980s disco music.

On the second day, we visited Asakusa and the Skytree. The Skytree is essentially similar to Tokyo Tower, so we didn’t go up either. Instead, we spent a long time wandering around Senso-ji Temple and the adjacent shopping street, buying some omamori (protective amulets) as souvenirs for friends back home. We drew lots at Senso-ji Temple. My friend drew an unlucky lot and tied it to a designated post in the temple. I was a little nervous, but ultimately drew a lucky lot and took the paper home with me. We had a very delicious all-you-can-eat grilled meat buffet at Yakiniku King (焼肉きんぐ) in the Asakusa shopping street, which was recommended by my Japanese teacher. Although it was a bit pricey, we ate a lot and treated it as two meals. In the evening, we took a walk around the Imperial Palace East Gardens—again, the leaves were dyed yellow by the lights, but fortunately they were ginkgo leaves, so the yellow hue looked natural. There are some parks within the perimeter of the Imperial Palace that visitors can enter, but the inner area is off-limits. To me, it just felt like a larger traditional Japanese castle with a dry moat, no main keep, and still in use.

The third day was the most anticipated: Mount Fuji. To save time, we bought a limited express bus ticket from Shinjuku to Kawaguchiko. However, unexpected traffic jams meant it took us two and a half hours to reach Kawaguchiko. The scenery was indeed stunning—the mountains in the suburbs of Tokyo were painted in six colors, with beautiful autumn foliage! We were lucky: it was cloudy when we woke up, but a corner of the sun peeked out during the journey, and by evening the sky cleared completely, like a sleepy college student finally waking up. At noon, seeing Mount Fuji covered by clouds, we thought there was no snow on the mountain. But once the sky cleared, we saw that there was already some snow on Mount Fuji. I brought a film camera, and I used almost all 36 shots while at Mount Fuji. The return trip to central Tokyo took another three and a half hours. We wanted to go to Noboritei, another restaurant recommended by my Japanese teacher, to eat unagi don (grilled eel rice), but due to traffic, we missed its operating hours. We considered going to Ichiran Ramen, which is open 24 hours, but found that even at 11 p.m., it was still packed, so we gave up. In the end, we bought a taiyaki (fish-shaped cake filled with red bean paste) and picked up some hot food from FamilyMart to make a quick meal. Surprisingly, the chicken cutlet and meat buns at FamilyMart tasted much better than those in China, exceeding my expectations and leaving me quite satisfied.

On the last day, we wanted to visit the Ghibli Museum, but learned the night before that reservations must be made at least 4 days in advance, so we had to cancel the plan. Since my friend and I had different flight schedules and wanted to explore different places, we decided to go our separate ways. I visited two Japanese gardens close to the hotel: the Old Hamarikyu Gardens and the Old Shiba Imperial Gift Garden. I also went to Shiba Park to see Tokyo Tower during the day. Both Japanese gardens were very quiet, with few visitors. Looking at the distant skyscrapers, there was no noise around me—only the faint chirping of birds, which calmed my mind. My first thought was why the parks in China aren’t maintained as well as these gardens. Then I remembered that Qiu Xia Pu and Hui Longtan near my home are also similar urban oases—I should visit those places more often.

I wanted to have lunch at Noboritei or Ichiran Ramen, which I didn’t get to try the day before, but looking at the time, I had to rush to Narita Airport and had lunch there. Boarding the plane, I looked back on this short holiday and then firmly returned to my daily routine. The airline meal prepared for passengers at Narita Airport was delicious—a final pleasant surprise. Life went on as usual, just like before.

Capsule Hotel

I originally planned to travel alone, but my friend decided to join me after I booked the flights and hotel. So even though we were two people, we stayed in a capsule hotel—which is the most cost-effective option for solo trips. I first learned about capsule hotels from videos by the YouTuber Zhuangzi. Capsule hotels are a unique Japanese accommodation model; although they are essentially dormitory rooms, I learned from videos that they are well-maintained. Additionally, many foreign solo travelers stay in capsule hotels, allowing them to interact with guests from all over the world, much like a global hostel. So I wanted to give it a try.

The capsule hotel I chose was a chain called 9h Nine Hours. I picked the Hamamatsucho branch in Minato Ward because the rooftop of that store offers a view of Tokyo Tower. When checking in, the staff gave us towels and slippers, and earplugs could be taken freely. After learning it was our first time staying there, the staff provided us with a Chinese version of the instructions, and then we went upstairs.

The hotel has 10 floors, and men and women’s areas are separated starting from the elevator. The floors accessible to men are 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, and 10.

  • On the 2nd floor are the luggage room, public bath, and sinks, along with 4 restrooms. The public bath and sinks are very abundant; I never encountered a queue during my stay. The bath is large, with separate wet and dry areas, and plenty of space to place clothes. In the luggage room, each person has a small compartment. You can put your suitcase in the space under the compartment; if it doesn’t fit, you can leave it at the front desk. The compartments are a bit small, making it inconvenient to store bags and clothes.
  • Floors 4, 5, 7, and 8 are bedroom floors. I stayed in a bunk bed on the 4th floor, unit 406. The space in the bunk bed compartment is actually quite large—you can sit upright, the light can be adjusted, and you can draw the curtain. The only downside is the lack of space to store personal items, and there’s only one socket, so it’s best to bring a power strip. The sound insulation of the bunk bed isn’t great; I could hear the snoring of the person in the next bed—hence the usefulness of the free earplugs. The items in the bunk bed need to be cleared every morning for cleaning; the next evening, you’ll find new pajamas and towels on the bed. The pajamas were a bit small for me, so I never wore them and returned them to the front desk. There’s a small balcony on the bedroom floors. Outside the bathroom stall, there’s an independent door that makes no noise when opened or closed, which is very quiet. The guests are generally very polite, and the entire bedroom floor is very quiet. The only slightly impolite thing I noticed was that I was woken up by an alarm clock almost every morning—there was only one such alarm, and it rang for a long time several times, presumably without waking the owner. Most people use vibrating alarms.
  • The 10th floor is a lounge with sockets, tables, and chairs. There are always many people here, but there’s enough space to find a seat. Everyone is quite quiet, like in a café, perhaps because we’re not familiar with each other. There’s a staircase on the 10th floor leading to the rooftop, where you can overlook Tokyo Tower—but the view isn’t as good as expected. The seats on the rooftop are metal and a bit cold, and since there aren’t many people, it’s easier to strike up a conversation there.

When checking out, you just need to leave the room card at the front desk. However, this hotel doesn’t allow luggage storage after checkout—although no one actually checks, as a polite Chinese person, I still sent my luggage to the station; almost every station offers luggage storage services.

People I Met

Tokyo is an international city, and since the hotel I stayed in was like a hostel with capsule rooms, I was very eager to interact with people from all over the world. Thanks to my friend’s outgoing personality, we did indeed chat with many people.

The first person we talked to was on the subway on the first night back at the hotel. My friend was meditating and accidentally made eye contact with a woman across from us. When she saw the Chikawa merchandise in our duty-free bag, we started chatting. We didn’t discuss anything profound; we tried to communicate in our broken Japanese, stumbling along, but we could understand each other. The woman wished us a pleasant trip, but in fact, due to our conversation, we accidentally rode past our stop.

That night, my friend and I went to the rooftop to see Tokyo Tower and struck up a conversation with a young man from Saudi Arabia. He had started working right after graduating from undergraduate school and was now on his annual leave. He had 20 days of annual leave, all of which he used for a trip to Japan. We were surprised he had 20 days of leave; he argued that China should have the same. In reality, after checking, we found that Chinese workers with 2–10 years, 10–20 years, and over 20 years of experience only get 5, 10, and 15 days of statutory annual leave, respectively. We talked about academics; I said I might pursue a Ph.D. He said he thought work experience was more important than studying in school, so he started working early. I actually agreed with this view, as I also felt the efficiency of learning in school is very low. But I explained to him that in China, it’s very difficult to find a good job without a master’s or Ph.D. degree, so even though I agreed with his view, I had to finish my Ph.D. before working.

On the first night, my friend discovered there was a problem with his phone’s SIM card and needed to remove and reinsert it. Unfortunately, he had lost his SIM ejector tool. I suggested he ask at Docomo downstairs. We went to Docomo early the next morning. Ironically, there was a large sign in the Docomo store saying, “No tourist SIM cards for sale.” We originally planned to queue, but another staff member came over. We asked if they could give or sell us a SIM ejector tool; he initially refused. But when we told him my friend’s phone had issues and needed the tool frequently, he hesitated for a moment and took one from the bunch of ejector tools and gave it to us. “Give you,” he said, making an exception for us.

We ran into the Saudi guy on the rooftop again the next evening, but didn’t talk much. I had a long chat with a British guy. He looked to be in his thirties and was a mechanic. He said he flew 14 hours to come to Tokyo. Even old developed countries are wealthy—even a mechanic can take a two-week trip to a place 14 hours away by plane. This wasn’t his first time in Tokyo; he liked it so much that he even considered settling there, but language wasn’t his strong suit, and he couldn’t learn Japanese. We talked a lot. He believed that regardless of the government, ordinary people are always good—including British, Japanese, and Chinese people. He said Japan was developing rapidly; he stayed at this same hotel last year, and the building next to it hadn’t started construction yet, but now it was almost finished. We laughed and said China’s infrastructure development speed is even more impressive, though the design of Chinese buildings is indeed inferior to Japan’s. Since he was a mechanic, we also talked a lot about cars. He mentioned that Chery wants to enter the UK market; although Chery is seen as a low-end brand in China, he said Chery aims to target the premium segment in the UK. He works for Volvo (now owned by Chery), and he said for the same configuration, Chery is much cheaper than Volvo, which interested him. We also discussed whether electric vehicles are truly environmentally friendly—there’s a theory that the West doesn’t develop EVs because electricity production itself is not eco-friendly. The British guy thought EVs are environmentally friendly—at least they don’t emit exhaust gases.

On the third day, we went to Mount Fuji. While taking photos at Mount Fuji, we met another group of Chinese tourists. We took Polaroid photos of each other. My friend and I were even mistaken for a same-sex couple. At a spot with beautiful autumn foliage, some Japanese grandmothers and aunties passing by offered to take a photo of my friend and me. Their English wasn’t very fluent, and our Japanese was very poor, so we communicated mostly through gestures, mixed with Japanese and English. But it was clear they were very kind and welcoming to tourists—even though the Chinese and Japanese governments have tense relations, ordinary people remain friendly. When leaving, my friend struck up a conversation with a French guy on the beach. This was his graduation trip; he had been traveling alone in Japan for nearly a month. He was leaving on Monday, just like us, flying first to Shanghai and then transferring home. That night, my friend and I got stuck in traffic on the way back and ended up looking for dinner in Shimbashi. Seeing that the Ichiran Ramen in Shimbashi also had a long queue at midnight, my friend and I were stunned. At that moment, a Chinese woman heard us speaking Chinese and approached us, saying Ichiran Ramen was now like a restaurant. So we started searching for other restaurants on Dianping. She then asked if we wanted to drink; I said no, we were just looking for dinner. Only then did I realize that she and her friend beside her were actually sex workers. She seemed a little embarrassed and told us, “There’s a lot to eat there,” so we parted ways. She was quite nice, though meeting her in this way was a bit awkward.

On the last day, I didn’t meet anyone particularly memorable in Japan, except that the Japanese official at the boarding gate spoke Chinese and said, “Thank you.” After landing, at the entrance to the video chat area in Pudong Airport—the signage was really poorly done. An elderly Shanghai lady was also looking for it, so we started talking, and later chatted via video chat together. She looked like the typical old intellectual, with elegant manners and clothing. Her son immigrated to Germany, and she was at Pudong Airport sending her grandson to Germany. When talking about China and Japan, she said, “People who have been abroad and seen the outside world won’t think this is a big deal.” I couldn’t agree more. I forgot what else we talked about, but her mannerisms and views left me wanting to become someone like her.

Reflections

Before going to Tokyo, I was skeptical about Tokyo being called the “largest city in Asia.” I always thought Shanghai, Hong Kong, and Singapore could compete with it. But after visiting, I was truly convinced. Tokyo’s buildings aren’t very tall, but the urban area is extremely vast. There’s no off-season in Tokyo; even in November, Shinjuku streets were still crowded with tourists. Tokyo’s internationalism also surpasses that of Shanghai, Hong Kong, and even Singapore—you can really see black and white staff working at FamilyMart. There’s still a big gap between Shanghai and Tokyo. The road ahead is long!
