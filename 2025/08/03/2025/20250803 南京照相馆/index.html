按下快门，扣下扳机

第一次发现按下快门和扣下扳机这么像。打开机身，打开枪膛；放入胶片，塞进子弹；按下快门，扣下扳机：前者定格世界，后者定格生命——都是永恒，不过前者创造，后者湮灭。

影片的开头就用这一段蒙太奇展示了两者的相似性，伊藤秀夫拍下杀人的照片，日军新兵杀人。伊藤秀夫眼中似乎有同情与震撼，甚至还“救”了阿昌——他叫阿昌“朋友”，给阿昌报名表通行证，看似同情中国的动摇者、庇护中国人的善人——影片开头似乎在用尽方法欺骗说服观众：伊藤秀夫是好人。伪善的伏笔暗藏其中：梦想是继承参加甲午战争的爷爷攻入中国、通行证上面的“有效期两日”。或许他有过人性中朴素的对恶行的排斥与对自己所做的动摇，我不认为他毫无人性，但军国主义将他洗脑让他罔顾人性的忠告，他从未正视人性与自己所做所为的矛盾，陷入阿伦特所说的“无思”的状态。

照相馆是一个很巧妙的切入点，在这个特殊的时候，照片的媒介必须通过照相馆才能刊出，主角一行也由此看到了真实的、血淋淋的南京。但更加令人触目的是，照片里的不是一群陌生人，而是一群街坊邻居。昨日身边活生生的人，成了今日照片里惨死的人，让人感到倒吸凉气但又愤怒——老金说：“我们不能当汉奸。”于是这一行人里，没有一个汉奸， 林毓秀和小孩活了下来，传出了照片；老金妻女死得意外，且因死亡毫无意义，更加令人扼腕愤怒；老金与阿昌用自己的方式，物理、精神上都算是出了口恶气；宋存义用自己的生命换来了其他人的活命，用南京城墙砸死了日本人。只有汉奸死得像狗一样。

《鬼子来了》中，日本军队在影片开头给小孩糖，在最后杀光了小孩。伊藤秀夫一开始给了阿昌生存——连给生存的权利竟然都能算是恩赐了，甚至或许真的对于阿昌产生了一种近似友谊的情感。但是在长官的催促下，在他自己的军国主义思想下，他拒绝了人性，并且要用杀死阿昌来彻底斩断自己的人性。可怜老金至死也不知道自己的妻女已经死了。伊藤秀夫最后冲进照相馆的时候依然在撒谎：“我当你是朋友。”无牵挂无生路的阿昌说实话：“我们不是朋友。”一句“中日亲善”贯穿始终。

一般来说，我不爱看主旋律抗战片——反而是被一些手撕鬼子的无端滑稽和强行升华的价值观消磨掉了忍耐力。好的电影想要传达它的情感与价值不需要直白地喊出来，而是让观众自己滑向那种情感，自己解读自己的价值。南京照相馆最后的煽情使用了大量蒙太奇——照片本身就是很好的蒙太奇的桥梁。老金换进伊藤秀夫胶卷的南京市民日常胶卷和南京市民被杀的镜头不断切换，普通生活的美好以这样一种方式在观众眼前被撕碎。日常胶卷掺杂在屠杀胶卷里，伊藤秀夫看后暴怒——被撕碎的美好用这样一种方式完成了自杀式的反击，杀死了伊藤秀夫。伊藤秀夫曾说：“支那人的照片不重要。”支那人的照片杀死了他，他自己的照片杀死了日本军官。

《辛德勒的名单》中，德国军官曾试图枪杀犹太人，扣下扳机之后几次发现枪坏了，最终不杀了，最终他也类似地像个玩具一样被杀死。同样是报应，胜利后，还是在南京，曾经草菅人命的日本军官在同一个地方被枪毙，同样是快门和扳机，这次照片和子弹成功相遇了。

片尾曲，昔日的南京照片放到今日同样的地方，如今国泰民安，不见昔日日寇，只见车水马龙，感慨万分。

也在想，这部电影有可能出海嘛？电影本身质量不低，《辛德勒的名单》、《拯救大兵瑞恩》甚至《壮志凌云》都能够在国际上享有自己的话语权，那《南京照相馆》有可能嘛？

看完电影，骑车回家，下大暴雨了，淋了一身湿——也好久没有淋过雨了。上海在下雨，南京也在下雨吧。

Press the shutter, pull the trigger

I first realized how similar pressing a camera shutter is to pulling a gun trigger. Open the camera body, open the gun chamber; insert film, load bullets; press the shutter, pull the trigger: the former freezes a moment of the world, the latter freezes a life — both create eternity, but the former gives birth to something new while the latter leads to annihilation.

The film opens with this montage to show the similarity between the two: Ito Hideo takes photos of killings, while a Japanese recruit commits murder. There seems to be sympathy and shock in Ito Hideo’s eyes; he even “saves” A Chang — calling him “friend” and giving him a registration form and pass. He appears to be a compassionate figure who takes pity on Chinese collaborators and protects the Chinese. The opening of the film seems to go to great lengths to deceive and persuade the audience: Ito Hideo is a good person. Hidden clues of hypocrisy lie within: his dream is to follow in the footsteps of his grandfather who fought in the First Sino-Japanese War and invaded China, and the pass has an expiration date of just two days. Perhaps he once had a simple aversion to evil acts and hesitated about what he was doing; I do not think he was completely devoid of humanity. But militarism brainwashed him into ignoring the warnings of humanity. He never confronted the contradiction between his humanity and his actions, falling into what Hannah Arendt called a state of “thoughtlessness”.

A photo studio is a very clever narrative entry point. At that particular time, photos could only be published through a photo studio, allowing the protagonists to see the real, bloody reality of Nanjing. What is even more shocking is that the people in the photos are not strangers, but neighbors. People who were alive and well yesterday became the victims in today’s photos — a sight that makes one gasp in horror and fury. Lao Jin says, “We cannot be traitors.” Thus, none of this group are traitors. Lin Yuxiu and the child survive and manage to spread the photos; Lao Jin’s wife and daughter die in a senseless, meaningless way, which is even more infuriating. Lao Jin and A Chang take revenge in their own ways, both physically and mentally. Song Cunyi sacrifices his life to save the others, using the Nanjing city wall to crush the Japanese soldiers. Only the traitors die like dogs.

In Devils on Horseback, the Japanese army gives candy to children at the beginning of the film, then kills all the children at the end. Ito Hideo initially grants A Chang the right to survive — even granting the right to live is treated as a favor, and he may even have developed a friendship-like feeling for A Chang. But under the urging of his superiors and his own militarist ideology, he rejects humanity and chooses to kill A Chang to completely sever his humanity. Poor Lao Jin never knows until his death that his wife and daughter have already perished. When Ito Hideo rushes into the photo studio at the end, he still lies: “I consider you my friend.” A Chang, with no ties and no way out, tells the truth: “We are not friends.” The phrase “Sino-Japanese friendship” runs through the entire film.

Generally, I am not fond of mainstream anti-Japanese war films — I have lost patience with their nonsensical slapstick scenes of ordinary people fighting off Japanese invaders and forced moral preachings. A good film does not need to shout its emotions and values outright; instead, it lets the audience be drawn into those emotions and interpret the values for themselves. The emotional climax of Dead to Rights uses extensive montage techniques — photos themselves serve as excellent bridges for montage. The footage of daily life of Nanjing citizens that Lao Jin replaces Ito Hideo’s film with alternates continuously with shots of the citizens being killed. The beauty of ordinary life is torn apart before the audience in this way. When the daily life footage is mixed with the massacre footage, Ito Hideo flies into a rage — the torn-apart beauty thus carries out a suicidal counterattack, killing Ito Hideo. Ito Hideo once said, “Photos of the Chinese are unimportant.” Photos of the Chinese kill him, and his own photos kill the Japanese officer.

In Schindler’s List, a German officer tries to shoot a Jew; after pulling the trigger several times and finding the gun jammed, he ultimately decides not to kill, and he too is killed like a toy. It is the same kind of retribution: after victory, still in Nanjing, the Japanese officer who once treated human lives as worthless is shot dead in the same place. Again, it is the shutter and the trigger — this time, the photo and the bullet meet successfully.

In the ending theme song, old photos of Nanjing are shown against the backdrop of the same location today. Now, the country is peaceful and prosperous; there are no traces of the Japanese invaders, only bustling traffic. It is truly moving.

I also wonder: Is this film likely to be released overseas? The film itself is of high quality. Films like Schindler’s List, Saving Private Ryan, and even Top Gun have gained international recognition. Could Dead to Rights achieve the same?

After watching the film, I rode my bike home and was caught in a heavy downpour, getting drenched — it had been a long time since I last got caught in the rain. It is raining in Shanghai; it must be raining in Nanjing too.
