2025 WWDC 小谈

说来好笑，正是因为复习量子物理到深夜，我才得以观看2025WWDC的直播。这是我第一次看WWDC的直播，第二次看苹果发布会的直播（上一次是“scary fast”)。对于这次WWDC，我觉得还算是满意吧，虽然没怎么回答我想问的问题，但是至少还算是给了一份答卷。

Apple Intelligence——失望与期望

2024年的苹果发布会，画了一张巨大的大饼——Apple Intelligence。这是苹果近年来唯一的一次食言，起了个大早，赶了个晚集。去年刚发布的时候，这看上去又是“Only Apple can do”，提出了既能保护用户隐私又能帮助用户的人工智能的结构概念，都以为是又一次苹果为一片混沌的新领域证明正确的发展和应用的方向，但结果却是一场惨痛的滑铁卢，甚至让苹果背上“虚假宣传”的骂名。

目前为止，我听到的消息来自林亦LYi，认为他讲得还是较为详细有理。简言之苹果机器学习的高管John Giannandrea面对ChatGPT的横空出世不作为，没有跟上AI发展的浪潮，但又做足了面子工程，画了很多饼，目前机器学习相关团队被重组给了帅气迷人幽默的Craig Federighi（主管软件工程）和Mike Rockwell（Vision Pro总负责）。这两人由于John的不作为积怨已久，且专业能力管理能力均较强，我本以为今年可能就已经会有很大的改观，但是事实上并没有这么快。希望WWDC2026上苹果给出一份满意的答卷！

Apple Intelligence落后了吗？落后了！但是，我认同，现在也没有落后很多。因为其他厂商对于AI功能加入系统的方式仍然是一片混沌，并没有找到一种深刻影响工作效率的方法。而且仍然有许多亟待解决的伦理问题——例如荣耀MagicOS的“一句话点外卖”功能，是基于AI对于屏幕内容的识别，一方面这是对于隐私的侵犯；另一方面误操作的成本谁来负担，这都是目前没有伦理共识。所以，其实在这个时候各个厂商都有点踟蹰不前，因为有点没有前进的空间。但是苹果提出要端侧模型和云端模型并重，提出了一个合理的结构，规避了这个伦理问题，所以其实苹果是由前进空间，只是在科技方面有点落后了。诚然如此，但是苹果其实依然没有为伦理的讨论做出贡献，苹果只是规避了这个问题，纳入旧伦理的体系中去解决这个问题。

我比较感兴趣的Apple intelligence的理念反而是在WatchOS的部分提到的，里面提到一个关键词**“主动性”**，我认为这其实一针见血地指出了AI应用发展的一个重要方向。希望不要沦落到概念先行，从未落地的田地。

Liquid Glass——松了一口气

UI更新是本次发布会又一次比较重要的内容。本来听过太多rumor，见过太多丑丑的渲染图，祈祷过无数次不会改不要乱改，不要为了改而改。最终给出的答卷还算满意。最终听到的消息是要统一为Vision Pro的视觉语言，之后就被曲解为圆形图标——我为此担心了很久，直至被这个来自谐门东西的视频说服。简言之，圆形图标只有Vision Pro和Apple Watch的“无限平面”的设计语言才会出现，且为蜂窝状排列，不仅如此，Vision pro里的iPad兼容app还需要通过图标的方圆来区分。因此不可能改为圆形图标，一切只是以讹传讹。

最终的设计，我认为还是维持了苹果的审美的——甚至加入了透明app图标，这多像是概念版的iOS啊！（但是其实我不喜透明图标，因为丧失了颜色对于视觉的传达。）新的UI最被人诟病的是可读性的问题——透明让底层的内容影响了顶层内容的阅读。这个问题在这个四位up主对谈的视频里讨论过。新UI的实现分为四层——Lense层（基础的材质）、Diffusion层（让透视光线变柔和）、高光层（控制透明度）、 文字与UI。为了改善可读性，苹果官方的文档都指出可以调整Diffusion层与高光层。事实上，在视频播放器界面，暂停按钮（纯SVG图标）和+10s、-10s按钮（混合了文字的图标）已经做了可读性的区别。但是在控制中心，苹果对于可读性的控制就非常差——明明通过调整参数就可以解决。不知道是不是为了小小引起热度，还是纯粹是beta版系统不够完善。我看到的小道消息是，控制中心就是还没有做完。

（感觉对第三方未适配app的支持还不错）

这个UI的另外一个争议是对性能和续航的损耗。WWDC引出新UI的方式都是说“随着算力的提高”，而且这种材质和效果势必会增加性能与续航的压力。我看到的小道消息，说是并没有用到光追，但是也确实非常吃性能和能耗。就我自己而言，我本来想为了体验iPad版的Journal（事实上也并未上线），把我的A15 iPad mini 6升级至了Developer Beta，结果系统卡顿到了一个完全不可用的状态。我现在手持的还是续航尿崩的苹果小火龙——iPhone 15 pro，在极客湾续航排行榜中垫底，已经开始担心续航了。

iPadOS——史诗级提升

iPadOS红绿灯冒出来的那一刻，我就震惊了。

我认为桌面操作系统和移动端操作系统的根本区别有两个

  • 手动杀后台vs自动杀后台
  • 以文件为中心vs以应用为中心

其实这次iPadOS确实在这两个方向发力。

对于前者，iPadOS允许了应用保持在后台，但是它的逻辑其实是折中的：对于桌面操作系统来说，默认不自动杀后台；对于移动操作系统，默认自动杀后台；而对于这次的iPadOS，默认自动杀后台，但是程序可以调取API，申请自己不被杀掉的权限。这这种操作逻辑足够简单，保持了移动端方面管理窗口的优势；又足够精细，保持了桌面端管理进程的优势。期待实际使用的效果。

对于后者，传统来说，桌面操作系统的应用可以对所有的文件都可以方便地获得相关权限，移动操作系统的应用只能获取该应用下文件的相关权限，想要获得全局的权限就相对麻烦。新的iPadOS维持了这一点不变，但是改变了其表现形式——桌面系统的表现也是以文件为中心的，其桌面上是文件；移动操作系统是以应用为中心的，其桌面上是应用。但是新的iPadOS允许将文件固定到Docker栏上，这已经部分地把文件和应用放到同一个等级上了（虽然安卓早已可以）。没有实际体验过，不知道这种程度的对于文件的地位的提升是否足够生产力的发展。

所以，你的下一台电脑，是否还需要是一台电脑吗？

iPadOS更新至此，生产力的瓶颈就不是系统了，而是应用了，之前被系统困太久的厂商可以发力了，做出一些更加像样的iPad端桌面级App了。

但是，随之我们又有了另外一个问题，那Mac呢？

上文所提的四位up对谈视频中有人提出，Mac和iPad只是占据各自舒适的生态位，我对此不完全赞同。我对于它的最直觉的感受是，iPad采用更加接近Mac的逻辑反而使得Mac的逻辑更容易被大家接受，反而会促进大家对于Mac的接受。而iPad本身承担的是“触屏电脑”的地位——Mac从来不做触控屏，是因为那个角度触控很别扭，但是触控放到iPad形态的桌面端设备，反而就合理起来，不论是硬件形态还是系统上。

可喜可贺，可喜可贺！不知道二手的iPad pro会不会涨价。这下12.9英寸和13英寸的iPad，M系列芯片的Mac，全部都更加合理了。

3D照片——新的护城河？

之前up主两颗皮蛋制作过系列节目【苹果护城河】，其中就有一期讲的是HDR，深以为然。

我认为，苹果在影像方面的护城河，历来有三：

其一为实况：前些年不受到广泛支持，从各个app对其适配，推动安卓也对其适配，其中经过近10年；

其二为HDR：苹果的HDR管理真的最完善最简单，而安卓与Windows的HDR管理总是相当麻烦。现在印象里安卓已经做得不错了，对安卓不构成护城河了，但是Windows的HDR管理依旧一言难尽，HDR与SDR的区分做得很差（不过近年来不怎么用Windows尤其是高端Windows了，也只是印象，欢迎反驳）；

其三为视频：iPhone的视频能力就是一直都一骑绝尘：尤其是稳定的4k60Hz和防抖性能更是，还有一些电影效果之类的有意思的功能，至今应当仍为护城河（也是印象里，欢迎举反例）。

而今年增加了照片3D化的功能，我认为很有可能是新的护城河。我更新了beta版的iPadOS之后的体验还是相对来说满意的，虽然达不到Vision Pro的效果，虽然只有富有层次感/主体在照片中心/使用广角拍摄的效果较好，但是依然让我很激动，感觉照片真的比以前更加鲜活了。上文所提的四位Up对谈中提出，这不是简单的双眼视察，而是运用了近1，2年兴起的高斯泼溅技术，迅速地将其应用到了新系统，确实让人印象深刻。

A Brief Talk on WWDC 2025

Funny enough, it was because I was reviewing quantum physics late at night that I got to watch the 2025 WWDC livestream. This was my first time watching a WWDC livestream and my second time watching an Apple event livestream (the previous one was the “scary fast” event). Overall, I’m fairly satisfied with this WWDC: although many of my questions weren’t answered, Apple at least provided a response.

Apple Intelligence — Disappointment and Expectations

At Apple’s 2024 event, the company painted a huge picture with Apple Intelligence. This was the only time Apple failed to deliver on a promise in recent years; they got a late start despite early hype. When it was first announced last year, it seemed like another example of “Only Apple can do this”: a concept for AI that would both protect user privacy and assist users. Many thought it would once again prove Apple’s right path for developing and applying technology in a chaotic new field. But in reality, it turned into a disastrous flop, even earning Apple the reputation of “false advertising”.

So far, the information I’ve heard comes from Lin Yi LYi, whose analysis is quite detailed and reasonable. In short, John Giannandrea, Apple’s senior executive in machine learning (John Giannandrea), did nothing in response to the emergence of ChatGPT and failed to keep up with the AI development trend. Yet he put on a show of grand promises. Currently, the machine learning-related teams have been reassigned to the charismatic and humorous Craig Federighi (head of software engineering) and Mike Rockwell (in charge of Vision Pro). These two had long harbored resentment over John’s inaction, and both have strong professional and management skills. I thought we might see major improvements this year, but that didn’t happen as quickly as expected. Hopefully Apple will provide a satisfactory answer at WWDC 2026!

Is Apple Intelligence behind? Yes, it is! But I also agree that the gap isn’t that large now. Other manufacturers are still struggling to integrate AI features into their systems in a way that significantly boosts work efficiency. There are also many urgent ethical issues to resolve—for example, Honor’s MagicOS “order food with one command” feature relies on AI to recognize screen content. On one hand, this violates user privacy; on the other hand, it’s unclear who will bear the cost of accidental operations, as there’s no current ethical consensus on this. So, all manufacturers are currently hesitant to move forward, as there’s little room for progress. However, Apple proposed giving equal importance to on-device models and cloud models, creating a reasonable framework that avoids these ethical issues. Thus, Apple actually has room to advance, even if it’s a bit behind in terms of technology. That said, Apple still hasn’t contributed to the ethical discussion; it simply avoided the issue by incorporating it into existing ethical frameworks.

The Apple Intelligence concept that interests me most was mentioned in the watchOS section, where the keyword “proactivity” was used. I think this precisely points to an important direction for AI application development. Hopefully, it won’t just remain a concept that never materializes.

Liquid Glass — A Relief

UI updates were another major highlight of this event. I’d heard too many rumors, seen too many ugly renderings, and prayed countless times that Apple wouldn’t make unnecessary changes just for the sake of changing. The final result was satisfactory. The initial news was that the UI would adopt the visual language of Vision Pro, which was misinterpreted as circular icons—I worried about this for a long time until I watched this video by Xiemen Dongxi. In short, circular icons only appear in the “infinite plane” design language of Vision Pro and Apple Watch, and they’re arranged in a honeycomb pattern. Plus, iPad apps compatible with Vision Pro need to be distinguished from regular apps by their icon shape. So, it’s impossible for the UI to switch to circular icons; all those claims were just misinformation.

In my opinion, the final design maintains Apple’s aesthetic—it even includes transparent app icons, which looks like a conceptual version of iOS! (Though I personally dislike transparent icons, as they lose the visual impact of color.) The most criticized aspect of the new UI is readability: the transparency causes the underlying content to interfere with reading the top-layer content. This issue was discussed in the video Four Creators’ Discussion. The new UI consists of four layers: the Lense layer (basic material), Diffusion layer (softens transmitted light), Highlight layer (controls transparency), and text/UI elements. To improve readability, Apple’s official documentation notes that you can adjust the Diffusion and Highlight layers. In fact, in the video player interface, the pause button (a pure SVG icon) and the +10s/-10s buttons (icons with text mixed in) already have distinguishable readability. But in the Control Center, Apple’s control over readability is poor—something that could easily be fixed by adjusting parameters. I’m not sure if this is done to generate some buzz or if it’s simply due to the beta version being incomplete. The rumor I heard is that the Control Center isn’t finished yet.

(I find the support for third-party apps that aren’t yet optimized to be quite good.)

Another controversy surrounding this UI is its impact on performance and battery life. Apple promoted the new UI at WWDC by saying “as computing power improves”, and such materials and effects will inevitably increase the load on performance and battery. The rumor I heard is that Apple didn’t use ray tracing, but the UI still consumes a lot of performance and energy. Personally, I wanted to try the iPad version of Journal (which wasn’t released anyway), so I upgraded my A15-powered iPad mini 6 to the Developer Beta—only to find the system was so laggy it was unusable. I’m currently using an iPhone 15 Pro, a device with notoriously poor battery life that ranks at the bottom of Geekbench’s battery rankings, and I’m already worried about its battery performance.

iPadOS — An Epic Upgrade

I was shocked when the iPadOS traffic light feature appeared.

I think there are two fundamental differences between desktop operating systems and mobile operating systems:

  • Manual background process termination vs. automatic background process termination
  • File-centric vs. app-centric

This time, iPadOS indeed focused on both aspects.

Regarding the former, iPadOS allows apps to stay in the background, but the logic is a compromise: for desktop operating systems, background processes aren’t terminated by default; for mobile operating systems, they are terminated by default. For this iPadOS, background processes are terminated by default, but apps can call APIs to request permission to stay active. This operation logic is simple enough to retain the advantages of mobile systems in window management, while being precise enough to maintain the advantages of desktop systems in process management. I look forward to seeing how it performs in practice.

Regarding the latter, traditionally, apps on desktop operating systems can easily access all files, while apps on mobile operating systems can only access files within their own directory; getting global access is relatively difficult. The new iPadOS keeps this unchanged but alters its presentation: desktop systems are file-centric, with files on the desktop; mobile operating systems are app-centric, with apps on the desktop. But the new iPadOS allows files to be pinned to the Dock, partially elevating files to the same level as apps (though Android has supported this for a while). Without trying it myself, I’m not sure if this level of elevation of files’ status is sufficient to drive productivity growth.

So, do you still need a separate computer as your next device?

With these iPadOS updates, the bottleneck for productivity is no longer the system—it’s the apps. Manufacturers that were held back by the system for too long can now step up and create more decent desktop-level apps for iPad.

But then we have another question: what about the Mac?

Someone in the aforementioned Four Creators’ Discussion video argued that the Mac and iPad simply occupy their respective comfortable niches, and I don’t fully agree. My intuitive feeling is that iPad adopting a logic closer to the Mac makes the Mac’s logic easier for people to accept, thus promoting adoption of the Mac. iPad itself serves as a “touchscreen computer”—the Mac doesn’t have a touchscreen because touch interaction is awkward from that angle, but it makes sense for a desktop device in iPad form, both in terms of hardware and software.

What a relief! I wonder if the price of used iPad Pros will rise. Now, 12.9-inch and 13-inch iPads, as well as Macs with M-series chips, all make more sense.

Spatial Scenes — A New Moat?

Creator Two Century Eggs produced a series called 【Apple’s Moats], and one episode covered HDR, which I completely agree with.

I think Apple has three traditional moats in imaging:

The first is Live Photos: they weren’t widely supported in earlier years, but after various apps adapted to them, Android followed suit—a process that took nearly 10 years.

The second is HDR: Apple’s HDR management is truly the most complete and simplest, while HDR management on Android and Windows is always quite troublesome. I recall Android has improved a lot now, so it no longer forms a moat for Apple, but HDR management on Windows remains poor, with a bad distinction between HDR and SDR (though I rarely use Windows, especially high-end Windows versions these days, so this is just my impression; feel free to correct me).

The third is video: iPhone’s video capabilities have always been far ahead, especially its stable 4K60Hz recording and image stabilization, along with interesting features like Cinematic mode. These should still form a moat today (again, just my impression; feel free to provide counter-examples).

This year, Apple added the Spatial Scenes feature, which I think could become a new moat. My experience with the beta version of iPadOS was relatively satisfying: although it doesn’t match the effect of Vision Pro, and the best results come from photos with clear depth, a centered subject, or taken with a wide-angle lens, I’m still excited—photos feel much more vivid than before. As mentioned in the Four Creators’ Discussion, this isn’t simple binocular vision; Apple applied Gaussian splatting, a technology that emerged in the past 1-2 years, to the new system quickly, which is truly impressive.
